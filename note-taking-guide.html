The Note-Taking Guide

Good notes are not a copy of the lesson. They are a tool for thinking now and for testing yourself later.

1Why handwriting often helps

Writing by hand is slower than typing, which forces you to summarise and choose what matters. That act of processing is part of learning. If you type, try to write in your own words rather than transcribing.

2The Cornell method

Divide the page into three parts:

  1. Notes column (right, wide): main ideas during the lesson or reading.
  2. Cue column (left, narrow): questions or keywords added after class.
  3. Summary (bottom): two or three sentences in your own words.

To revise, cover the notes column and answer the questions in the cue column.

3Choosing a format

FormatBest for
CornellLectures and textbook chapters
OutlineWell-structured topics with clear headings
Mind mapConnections between ideas, brainstorming essays
Table or matrixComparing several items on the same features
Flow chartProcesses, cycles and cause and effect

4What to write down

  • Definitions and key terms, in your own words.
  • Examples the teacher gives, because they show how ideas are used.
  • Anything repeated or emphasised.
  • Questions you have, marked with a symbol to follow up.

5Review within 24 hours

Spend ten minutes the same day tidying your notes, adding cue questions and writing the summary. It is far easier while the lesson is fresh, and it counts as your first spaced review.

6Turn notes into tests

Every heading can become a question. Every list can become “name the five…”. Notes that you can quiz yourself on are worth far more than notes you only re-read.
